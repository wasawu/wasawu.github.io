Byzantine Study Guide

Unit 9: The Byzantine Empire, the Islamic Empire, and the Crusades. Covers the packet (History Alive, Lessons 28 to 30).

1. The Byzantine Empire

Key idea: geography made Constantinople rich and hard to attack.

  • Also called: the Eastern Roman Empire. It lasted from 395 to 1452 CE and included parts of Europe, Asia, and Africa.
  • Capital: Emperor Constantine moved the capital from Rome to Byzantium in 330 CE. The city was renamed Constantinople. Today it is Istanbul.
  • Why the location worked: the city sat on a peninsula with a great harbor. It had access to the Black Sea and Mediterranean ports. Whoever controlled Constantinople and the Bosporus Strait controlled trade between Europe and Asia.
  • Defenses: water on three sides; a 500-yard chain stretched across the harbor; a three-tier wall system about 13 miles long with 96 towers; a moat.
  • Water: Emperor Valens built an aqueduct about 400 miles long. Water was stored under the city in the Basilica Cistern, which had a 125-foot ceiling and covered about 27 Olympic pools.
  • Culture: the empire was Christian (Emperor Theodosius I made the Roman Empire Christian), used Roman law and technology, and kept Greek language, culture, and literature alive. That is why we know so much about Greece and Rome today.
  • Decline: the Western Roman Empire fell into decay and ruin, while the East grew rich from trade.

2. Justinian and Theodora

Key idea: Justinian's law code is still the basis of many legal systems.

  • Justinian (483 to 565 CE): born a peasant, rose through the ranks of the army, and became the greatest Byzantine emperor.
  • Theodora: a former performer who married Justinian and co-ruled with him. Justinian's wife was strong, intelligent, and ruthless. She is credited with laws that helped the poor, the homeless, and slaves, with starting orphanages, and with championing women's rights.
  • Justinian's Code: he organized and simplified the complex Roman law into a clear code. It became the basis of modern Western law and of many other legal systems.
  • Public works: the Nika riots (532 CE) burned parts of Constantinople, so Justinian rebuilt them. He built churches and public buildings, including the Hagia Sophia, which still stands in Istanbul.
  • Wars: he wanted to restore Rome's former glory and fought the barbarian tribes. His armies were successful, but the empire could not hold the lands after his death.

3. The Split of the Christian Church

Key idea: the Roman Catholic Church and the Eastern Orthodox Church split in stages.

Christianity started as one church. Differences grew until the church split into two: the Roman (Catholic) church with its pope in Rome, and the Eastern Orthodox church with its patriarch in Constantinople.

4. The Arabian Peninsula and Muhammad

Key idea: harsh geography kept Arabs in clans and tribes, but shared religion and language united them.

  • Geography: the desert climate and lack of rain made farming hard. Arabs were not united like the city-states of Greece. They lived in clans or tribes.
  • Cities: each Muslim city centered on a souk (marketplace) and a mosque (place of prayer). Cities were centers of trade, government, education, and culture.
  • Farming: Arabs overcame the dry land through irrigation and by growing crops that could survive the climate, such as wheat, rice, beans, melons, almonds, figs, and olives.
  • Women: women got their social status from their father or husband. They could own property, trade, and get an education.
  • Muhammad: born into the divided Arabian Peninsula, he worked as a trader and merchant. He married Khadijah, who proposed to him. Around age 40 he began receiving messages from the angel Gabriel (Jibril). The Qur'an was written down later, after Muhammad's death.
  • Caliph: the Muslim ruler who came to power after Muhammad died.
  • Shared faiths: Islam, Judaism, and Christianity all believe in one God and trace their origin back to the prophet Abraham. All three include important figures such as Adam, Noah, Moses, and Jesus.
  • Two key items for Muslims: the Qur'an (holy book) and the Sunnah (how Muslims should practice their religion). Both provide guidance for the Five Pillars of Faith.

5. The Islamic Empire

Key idea: the empire grew fast, then split between dynasties and religious groups.

  • Umayyad Dynasty: the caliphs spread Islam. They let conquered people keep their own religion and did not force conversion, unlike the Byzantine and Persian empires. A split then formed between the Sunnis and the Shi'as. Shi'as believe only descendants of Ali ibn Abi Talib (Muhammad's son-in-law) have the right to rule. Sunnis accepted the Umayyad clan. Today about 85% of Muslims are Sunni.
  • Abbasid Dynasty (750 to 1258 CE): non-Arab Muslims rebelled in the 700s to get better jobs and lower taxes. The dynasty was centered in Baghdad and reached its high point, with a golden age of learning and trade.
  • Seljuk Turks: Central Asian people who invaded in 1055 CE and took control, but ruled as sultans while the Abbasid caliphs kept influence over religion. They expanded into Syria and Palestine and came into conflict with the Byzantine Empire, which led to the Crusades.
  • End: the Mongols invaded and sacked Baghdad in 1258 CE, ending the Abbasid empire.

6. The Crusades and Jerusalem

Key idea: three faiths hold Jerusalem holy, which is why it was fought over.

  • Causes of the Crusades: Christian Europe wanted control of the Holy Land. Seljuk Turks had taken the region and attacked Christians, which led the Christian church to call for crusades. Jerusalem was where Jesus was crucified.
  • Holy to Christians: Jesus was crucified and buried in Jerusalem.
  • Holy to Muslims: Muhammad ascended to heaven from Jerusalem. The Dome of the Rock marks this place.
  • Holy to Jews: the Great Temple once stood in Jerusalem. The Western Wall is the remaining wall of the Jewish Temple site.
  • Saladin (Salah al-Din): the Seljuk leader captured Jerusalem from the Christians in 1187 CE. In the Third Crusade he fought King Richard I of England to a stalemate.
  • Effects: the Crusades increased violence and mistrust between faiths, but they also brought trade and new ideas between Europe and the Muslim world.

7. Trade and Cultural Diffusion

Key idea: diffusion means ideas spread when cultures interact through trade, travel, and contact.

  • Location: Islamic lands sat between Europe, Africa, and Asia. Trade routes crossed the Mediterranean, the Red Sea, the Indian Ocean, and the Silk Road.
  • What traveled: ideas, math, science, technology, religion, foods, and goods.
  • Route examples: Arab traders crossed the Mediterranean Sea to reach Europe. A trader from Mecca to Cairo crosses a desert, while a trader from Mecca to Baghdad travels a different overland route. The southern boundary of Islamic lands in Africa was the Sahara Desert.

8. Muslim Innovations and Achievements

Key idea: Muslim scholars preserved Greek learning and added new discoveries.

Cities and learning

  • Baghdad: one of the largest cities in the world at the time, with about 1,000,000 people.
  • Mosques: had a dome, a tall tower (minaret), and a fountain for washing.
  • Scholarship: Muslim scholars translated Greek texts and studied them. Philosophers followed the example of Greek scholars such as Aristotle. Paper, libraries, and rulers who supported scholars helped learning grow.

Science and technology

  • Astronomy: improved the astrolabe and built observatories. Many star names in use today come from Arabic.
  • Zoology: scholars wrote detailed studies of animals, including turtles.
  • Water: irrigation systems, water wheels, and water clocks let people use scarce water.
  • Navigation: the astrolabe, the compass, guidebooks, and maps helped sailors and caravans find their way.

Mathematics

  • al-Khwarizmi: his books introduced algebra (the word comes from his book's title, al-jabr) and the Hindu-Arabic numerals to Europe, which replaced Roman numerals for calculation.
  • Zero: an Indian concept that Muslims helped spread. Zero stands for nothing, and it made place-value math possible.

Medicine

  • Hospitals: open to all, with doctors who treated patients.
  • Medication: pharmacies and medicines made from plants and chemicals.
  • Surgery: removing tumors, and eye surgery to remove cataracts.
  • Disease: antiseptics and the study of contagious diseases.
  • Ibn Sina (Avicenna): wrote The Canon of Medicine, a standard medical textbook used in Europe for centuries.

Literature, art, music, and recreation

  • Books: illuminated manuscripts were decorated with designs, pictures, and calligraphy.
  • Stories: One Thousand and One Nights had roots in India and Persia and spread through the Muslim world.
  • Art: decorative art used geometric and floral designs.
  • Textiles: valuable fabrics and clothing were important trade goods.
  • Music: Cordoba, Spain, had a new musical style that mixed Persian and Arab traditions.
  • Recreation: polo was a fast game played on horseback, popular with leaders and nobles. Chess was a game of intellect and strategy, a battle of wits with pieces that stood for an army.

10. Answer Key for the Packet Questions

Answers to the packet's questions, from the questions themselves.

Vocabulary page (Byzantine and Arabia)

  • Where was the Byzantine Empire located? Around Constantinople, in southeastern Europe and western Asia, on both sides of the Bosporus.
  • What city was important, and why? Constantinople, because of its great harbor and location on trade routes.
  • How was the city protected? Water on three sides, a chain across the harbor, and tall walls with towers.
  • Who is Justinian? The Byzantine emperor (527 to 565) who wanted to restore Rome and organized Roman law.
  • How was Theodora instrumental? She was his wife and co-ruler, who influenced laws and helped the poor.
  • Why is the Justinian Code important today? It is the basis of many modern legal systems.
  • Which cultures influenced Islamic civilization? Greek, Persian, Indian, Roman/Byzantine, and Egyptian cultures.
  • What were the Crusades? Christian military campaigns to take Jerusalem and the Holy Land from Muslim control.
  • Who eventually wins the Crusades? The Muslim forces. Saladin retook Jerusalem in 1187, and the Crusader states ended in 1291.

Byzantine Empire pages

  • What was the Byzantine Empire's religion? Christian. Theodosius I made the Roman Empire Christian.
  • Based on what? Roman law and technology, and Greek language, culture, and literature.
  • The Byzantine Empire flourished from 395 to 1452 CE and included portions of Europe, Asia, and Africa. Constantine moved the capital to Byzantium in 330 CE.
  • What made the city easy to defend and rich? Its location on a peninsula with a great harbor, water on three sides, and access to trade.
  • Constantinople walls: three-tier walls about 13 miles long with 96 towers.
  • How was the city's water supplied? By the 400-mile aqueduct built by Valens, and stored in the Basilica Cistern.

Justinian pages

  • What event forced Justinian to rebuild parts of Constantinople? The Nika riots of 532 CE, when fights and fires spread through the streets.
  • What were some improvements from his public works projects? Churches including the Hagia Sophia, public buildings, and water systems.
  • Why was Justinian's Code significant? It was the basis for many other law systems.
  • Justinian and Theodora were born as what? Peasants who rose to lead the empire. Theodora had worked as a performer.

Church split chart (Section 4)

  • 730 CE: Emperor Leo III and Pope Gregory III. Event: the ban on icons. Result: the pope opposed the ban.
  • 800 CE: Pope Leo III and Charlemagne. Event: the pope crowned Charlemagne Holy Roman Emperor after Byzantine Empress Irene refused to help Western Europe. Result: rival claims to the imperial title.
  • 1054 CE: The pope and the patriarch excommunicated each other. Result: the East-West Schism.

Lesson 28 reading notes

  • Why was Constantinople an ideal capital? It was surrounded by water, which made it easy to defend.

Lesson 29 (Arabian and Islamic notes)

  • Why did Islamic culture flourish though the empire did not last? Great cities made innovations, and scholars and artists shared ideas.
  • What is cultural diffusion? When different societies interact through trade, travel, or contact.
  • How did the location of Islamic lands make diffusion possible? They sat at the crossroads of trade routes between Europe, Africa, and Asia.

Lesson 29 (Section 2 to 10 questions)

  • Baghdad: a large city of about 1,000,000 people and a center of learning and trade.
  • Mosque design: a dome, a minaret, and a fountain.
  • Factors that promoted learning in Muslim lands: patronage by rulers, translation of Greek texts, paper, libraries, and wealth from trade.
  • Greek scholars: Muslim philosophers translated and studied Greek texts such as Aristotle's.
  • Navigation accomplishments: the astrolabe, the compass, and guidebooks and maps.
  • Why were al-Khwarizmi's books important? They introduced algebra and Hindu-Arabic numerals.
  • Why was the zero concept significant? It made place-value math possible.
  • Ibn Sina's Canon of Medicine: a standard medical textbook used in Europe for centuries.
  • Textiles: valuable fabrics and clothing that were major trade goods.
  • Chess and polo: chess is a game of intellect and strategy; polo is a fast game played on horseback.

Lesson 30 (Jerusalem)

  • Religions in the quarters: Muslim, Armenian (Christian), Christian, and Jewish.
  • Landmarks: the Temple Mount and Dome of the Rock (Muslim), the Church of the Holy Sepulchre (Christian), and the Western Wall (Jewish).
  • Why is Jerusalem special? It is holy to three religions and has many different people and holy sites.
  • Why might people fight over it? Control of holy sites for three religions.

Lesson 30 reading notes (Section 1)

  • Causes of the Crusades: Christians wanted the Holy Land; Seljuk attacks on Christians; the Christian belief that Jesus was crucified.
  • Holy to Christians: Jesus was crucified there.
  • Holy to Muslims: Muhammad ascended to heaven from Jerusalem.
  • Holy to Jews: The Great Temple once stood there.
